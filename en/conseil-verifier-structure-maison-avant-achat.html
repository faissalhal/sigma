Checking a house's structure before buying: 7 key points

The structure is the only trade that can make a house unsellable or dangerous. Here is what to look at and when to call in an engineer.

Published 16 November 2026 · 3 min read · SIGMA ICC, engineering consultancy

To check a house's structure before buying, inspect seven areas: cracks, columns and beams, floors, visible foundations, exposed steel, added storeys and retaining walls. If any of them shows a warning sign, bring in a structural engineer before signing: structural repairs often run into tens of thousands of dirhams.

The 7 points to inspect

  1. Cracks: note their shape, width and location. A fine crack in the render is common; a stepped crack in the masonry, a diagonal one from a window corner or one that goes right through the wall often indicates foundation movement.
  2. Columns and beams: they should be straight, with no spalling of the concrete. A column visibly thinner than the others or a beam sagging in the middle are serious signs.
  3. Floors: place a marble or a spirit level on the floor. A floor that "sounds hollow" or vibrates, or a ceiling with cracks parallel to the joists, should be examined.
  4. Visible foundations: in the basement, the crawl space or at the base of the façade, look for settlement, ground slabs pulling away from the walls and horizontal cracks at the bottom of walls.
  5. Exposed steel: concrete that spalls to reveal rusty bars indicates corrosion of the reinforcement, often due to insufficient cover or water ingress. Common on balconies and parapets.
  6. Added storeys: a floor built later on foundations designed for a single-storey house is a common situation in Morocco. Ask for the approved plans and read our article on adding a storey.
  7. Retaining walls and boundary walls: a wall that leans, cracks or seeps water on sloping ground can be very expensive to repair.

What the age of the house tells you

The RPS 2000 seismic code, revised in 2011, requires construction details (tie beams, minimum sections, reinforcement of joints) that many older houses do not meet. A house built before this code, or without an engineering firm, is not necessarily dangerous, but it deserves a more thorough assessment, especially if you plan to add a storey or open up a wall. See our article on RPS 2011.

The documents to ask for

  • The reinforced concrete drawings and, if possible, the original engineering firm's design calculations.
  • The approved plans from the building permit, to compare with what exists.
  • The soil survey, if one was carried out, particularly on clay soil or backfill.
  • Invoices for any repair work: a recently "repaired" crack must be explained.

What the structural engineer does

The engineer identifies the load-bearing system, measures the cracks, may carry out spot tests on the concrete (hardness, cover, position of the steel) and checks that the plans match the building. They conclude with a clear opinion: cosmetic defects, strengthening work to plan for with an estimate, or a risk that justifies walking away. Frequently asked questions

Which crack should worry you before buying a house?

A stepped crack, a diagonal one from the corner of an opening, one that goes right through, or one that widens over time. Fine hairline cracks in the render are generally of no structural significance.

Does an older house meet the seismic code?

Not necessarily: RPS 2000, revised in 2011, requires features that many older houses, or those built without an engineering firm, do not have. A structural assessment can check this.

Can you add a storey to a house you buy?

Only if the existing foundations, columns and floors can carry the additional load, which an engineer must verify by calculation, and with a building permit.
