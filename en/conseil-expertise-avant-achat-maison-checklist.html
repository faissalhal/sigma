House inspection before buying: the trade-by-trade checklist

A 30-minute viewing is not enough to judge a house. Here, trade by trade, is what a buyer should check, what should raise a red flag and what repairs typically cost.

Published 16 November 2026 · 4 min read · SIGMA ICC, engineering consultancy

Before signing a preliminary sale agreement, have the house inspected on eight points: structure, roof and waterproofing, damp, electrics, plumbing, drainage, joinery and administrative compliance. The first seven are a job for a building technician, the last one for the notary and the municipality. A defect spotted before signing can be used to negotiate the price or to walk away; discovered afterwards, it is yours to pay for.

The technical trades: where to look

  • Structure: walk around the outside, then through each room. Fine vertical cracks in render are often superficial; those that go through the wall, widen or start from the corners of openings deserve an engineer's opinion. Our article on cracks and how serious they are explains how to read them.
  • Roof and waterproofing: go up onto the roof terrace. In Morocco, most houses have a flat roof; waterproofing more than 10 to 15 years old and cracked upstands mean it will need redoing.
  • Damp: a moisture meter and a torch are enough for an initial check. Be wary of a room repainted just before the sale.
  • Electrics: open the consumer unit. An installation with no 30 mA residual current device and no earth connection needs to be redone, whatever the apparent condition of the sockets.
  • Plumbing and drainage: turn on all the taps at once, flush the toilets, watch how the water drains and read the meter before and after the viewing.
  • Joinery: operate every window and door; in Marrakech, single glazing and leaky frames weigh heavily on the air-conditioning bill.

The administrative side

Ask the seller for the land title and obtain a recent certificate of ownership from the land registry (ANCFCC): it shows the registered owner, mortgages and objections. The title proves ownership, not that the building is compliant: also ask for the building permit, the approved plans and the occupancy permit (permis d'habiter) provided for by Law 12-90 on urban planning, then compare the plans with what has actually been built.

Bringing in an engineering firm

A structural engineer and a building services technician can spot in one visit what a buyer cannot see: an undersized column, a sagging floor, a dangerous electrical installation. The report prices the work trade by trade and gives you an objective argument to negotiate. For ballpark figures, see our article on the cost of a building inspection. Frequently asked questions

What should you check before buying a house in Morocco?

Eight trades: structure, flat roof and waterproofing, damp, electrics, plumbing, drainage, joinery, and administrative compliance (land title, building permit and occupancy permit).

Is the land title enough to make the purchase safe?

No. The ANCFCC certificate of ownership proves who owns the property and lists mortgages, but it says nothing about whether the building is compliant: you also need the building permit, the approved plans and the occupancy permit.

When should the technical inspection be done?

Before signing the preliminary agreement or promise of sale, so that you can negotiate the price or walk away if the report reveals significant defects.
