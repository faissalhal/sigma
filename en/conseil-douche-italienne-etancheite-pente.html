Walk-in shower: slope, drain and waterproofing rules to follow

The level-access shower has become the norm in villas and riads. It is also one of the leading causes of water damage when it is poorly designed.

Published 16 November 2026 · 3 min read · SIGMA ICC, engineering consultancy

A successful walk-in shower relies on three things: a slope of at least 1% towards the drain (1 to 2 cm per metre, often 2% according to drain manufacturers), continuous waterproofing under the tiles, connected to the drain and turned up the walls, and a recess planned in the slab from the design stage. If any of these elements is missing, the water ends up in the slab or in the downstairs neighbour's home.

The slope

The CSTB guide on level-access showers adopts a minimum slope of 1% towards the drain in the shower area, and system manufacturers often require 2%. The instructions for the chosen waterproofing system take precedence. A slope that is too shallow leaves puddles; a slope that is too steep makes the shower slippery and complicates tile cutting.

  • Point drain: diamond-shaped slope towards the centre, with tiles cut accordingly.
  • Linear channel drain: a single slope, towards the wall or towards the entrance, compatible with large tiles.
  • Tileable ultra-flat shower tray: a prefabricated sloped base, safer if the tiler lacks experience.

Waterproofing under the tiles

Tiles and grout are not waterproof. A waterproof layer is therefore needed underneath: an under-tile water protection system (SPEC), in the form of a liquid membrane applied in several coats with corner tapes, or a bonded waterproof mat. DTU 52.2 states when this SPEC is required depending on the substrate; in a level-access shower on an upper floor, it is essential.

  • Connection of the waterproofing to the drain's flange or collar, using the parts provided by the manufacturer.
  • Upstands on the shower walls, to a sufficient height according to the instructions (at least where water splashes).
  • Reinforcing tapes in the corners, around pipe penetrations and at the shower threshold.
  • Flood test for watertightness before the tiles are laid.

The drain and the waste pipe

The floor drain must have a flow rate suited to the shower head (a high-flow rain shower can overwhelm a small drain), a sufficient water seal against smells, and be accessible for cleaning. The pipe connecting it to the stack must also keep a slope of at least 1 cm per metre (see drain slope). Sufficient height must therefore be provided: ultra-flat drain, recessed slab or thicker screed.

These defects are among the most documented building pathologies recorded by the Agence qualité construction in France.

To be planned from the drawing stage

A walk-in shower is decided before the slab is poured: a recess of a few centimetres in the shower area, the position of the drain, the routing of the waste pipe between the joists or ribs of the floor. The engineering firm coordinates these recesses with the structure, which avoids chiselling out a new slab or weakening a beam. If a leak is already present, see also detecting a hidden water leak.

Frequently asked questions

What slope for a walk-in shower?

At least 1% towards the drain, i.e. 1 cm per metre; many drain and system manufacturers require 2%, and the instructions for the chosen system take precedence.

Is waterproofing needed under the tiles of a walk-in shower?

Yes: tiles are not waterproof. A liquid membrane or waterproof mat (SPEC) connected to the drain and turned up the walls is essential, especially on an upper floor.

Can a walk-in shower be built without recessing the slab?

It is possible with an ultra-flat drain or a lifting pump, but the result is better when a recess is planned from the construction stage.
